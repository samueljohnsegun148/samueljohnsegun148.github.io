## Overview

Accessibility and safety are usually treated as separate disciplines. Accessibility teams build ramps and screen readers. Safety teams build evacuation plans and hazard protocols. They rarely talk to each other.

But for vulnerable populations — particularly blind and low-vision individuals — accessibility **is** a safety issue.

## The Argument

A blind person who cannot navigate a hospital independently faces real risks:
- Missed medical appointments due to wayfinding failure
- Increased pre-procedure anxiety that affects clinical outcomes
- Inability to self-evacuate during emergencies
- Loss of autonomy that leads to social isolation and mental health decline

These aren't inconveniences. They're health and safety failures.

## My Perspective

Having spent 9+ years in occupational Health, Safety & Environment before transitioning to accessibility research, I see the through line clearly. HSE asks: *"Can this person avoid harm in this space?"* Accessibility asks: *"Can this person use this space at all?"*

For the populations I serve, those are the same question.

## Implications

This framing has practical consequences:
- **Hospitals** should treat patient wayfinding as a safety metric, not a convenience feature
- **Building codes** should consider navigability for blind users as part of emergency preparedness
- **Technology companies** building indoor navigation should design for recovery, not just routing
- **Research funding** should recognize assistive navigation as a health and safety investment
